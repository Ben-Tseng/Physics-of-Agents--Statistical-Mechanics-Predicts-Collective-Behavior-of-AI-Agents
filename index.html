When 32 AI agents argue in a group chat, can we predict how it ends?

This paper says yes. A formula from physics that describes magnets can predict whether a group of interacting AI agents will agree, split into two camps, or stay undecided. This page walks you through it in ten steps, with simulations you can play with, assuming no background.

Batu El, …, Surya Ganguli, James Zou (Stanford)
Physics of Agents: Statistical Mechanics Predicts Collective Behavior of AI Agents. arXiv 2608.16578v2, 2026

Step 1

What the paper asks

Soon, many AI assistants will negotiate, schedule meetings, and allocate resources on our behalf. They will message each other and influence each other. A single reliable agent does not guarantee that a group of interacting agents will produce a reliable outcome.

The authors simplify this into a group chat: 32 agents discuss the same question. In each round, every agent reads the others' messages and states its position again, for 8 rounds in total. They want to answer three questions:

QuestionIn plain words
Final stateWill the group agree, split into two camps, or stay undecided?
Right or wrongDoes discussion bring the group closer to the right answer, or lead everyone into the same mistake?
PredictionKnowing only where each agent starts, can we predict who will change their mind?

Intuition

Two messages from friends were enough to turn this agent from "slightly agree" into "strongly disagree." The paper asks whether such changes follow rules that we can calculate.

Step 2

How the experiment works

Each AI group is built from four ingredients.

IngredientSetup in the paper
Members32 agents, each with a different persona. Four models are tested: GPT-4o-mini, Gemma-3n-E4B, Qwen3.5-9B, and Llama-3.1-8B.
QuestionsMath questions: competition problems turned into two-choice questions, with a correct answer. Each agent's persona is a worked solution to a different problem, which represents a different area of expertise.
Political statements: for example, "Stricter gun control laws would reduce violent crime rates." Agents agree or disagree; there is no correct answer. Personas come from profiles of real people.
NetworkEvery pair of agents is either friends, rivals, or unconnected. The relationship is written into the prompt, and messages arrive in a "friends" inbox or a "rivals" inbox.
Procedure8 synchronous rounds. In each round, every agent votes 5 times; the average is its opinion strength (from −1 to +1). It then writes a two-sentence message with one reason.
Example

An agent votes 5 times: agree, agree, disagree, agree, disagree. The average is (1+1−1+1−1)/5 = +0.2, meaning "slightly agree." If all 5 votes are agree, the opinion is +1, meaning "strongly agree." So an opinion can only take six values: −1, −0.6, −0.2, +0.2, +0.6, +1.

Remember

Total scale: 4 models × 60 questions × 10 networks × 4 repeats = 9,600 groups. With the extra experiments, more than 10,000 groups.

Step 3

The magnet analogy: agents as tiny compass needles

Physics has a classic model, about a century old, called the Ising model. It describes a set of tiny magnetic needles. Each needle points either up or down and is turned by its neighbors. The authors found that AI groups map onto it point by point.

World of magnetsWorld of AI groups
One tiny needleOne agent
Up or downAgree (+1) or disagree (−1)
Wants to point the same way as its neighborsWants to agree with friends
Wants to point opposite to some neighborsWants to oppose rivals
External magnetic fieldThe agent's own leaning, called the intrinsic field g (set by persona and question)
Temperature: hotter means more disorderRandomness: agents sometimes change their minds for no reason
Intuition

Every agent wants to feel as little tension as possible: it does not want to fall out with friends, and it does not want to go against its own leaning. The paper calls this tension "pressure"; physicists call it "energy." The system tends to move toward low-pressure states, but with some randomness.

Formula

The probability that agent i chooses "agree" in the next round:

P(agree) = σ( wfriend·(friends agreeing − friends disagreeing) + wrival·(rivals agreeing − rivals disagreeing) + gi )

σ is an S-shaped function. The larger the number inside, the closer the probability is to 1; the smaller, the closer to 0; at exactly 0 it is 50%.

The paper's key improvement is to split influence into three couplings: β⁺ (friends), β⁻ (rivals), and β₀ (simply being connected). The friend weight is then wfriend = β⁺ + β₀, and the rival weight is wrival = β₀ − β⁻.

Step 5

Five findings

Finding 1: Conviction grows as agents talk

Most groups start out undecided. After a few rounds, conviction rises steadily, for every model and every question type. The paper sorts group states into three regimes:

Remember

Indifference: the average position is near 0 and nobody is sure. Polarization: the average position is near 0, but everyone is sure; they have split into two camps. Consensus: everyone is sure and on the same side.

Finding 2: Most groups reach consensus instead of splitting

The fitted parameters show that the pull of friends is far stronger than the push of rivals. The effective friend weight (β⁺ + β₀) lies between 0.99 and 3.03. The effective rival weight (β₀ − β⁻) is at most 0.73, and for GPT and Qwen on political questions it is negative or close to zero. The push from rivals is too weak to hold two opposing camps apart.

Finding 3: On math questions, discussion improves accuracy

Group majorities switch from wrong to right much more often than from right to wrong. The reason: friends who hold the correct answer pull harder, and rivals who hold a wrong answer push harder. Both effects move the group toward the right answer.

Share of groups that switched between round 0 and round 8, read from Figure 5 of the paper.

Finding 4: On political questions, groups drift right

The share of right-leaning groups rises after discussion. The question set is balanced between left and right, so the drift is not caused by the questions. Discussion amplifies biases already built into the models.

Data from Appendix C.1 of the paper (pp.37–38). For Llama the paper only says it stays near 53% and ends marginally lower; 53 → 52 here is illustrative.

Finding 5: Behavior is predictable

Given only the starting positions, the model predicts round by round whether each agent will change its mind. See Step 7.

Types of trajectories

The paper sorts each agent's trajectory into 4 types by how many times it switches sides, and each group into 5 types by its majority at the start and the end.

Remember

Among individuals, "frozen" is the most common type, followed by "oscillator." Among groups, an initial majority is weakened or overturned surprisingly often, in 11–12% of groups for GPT-4o-mini and Qwen. So this is not simply "the majority wins."

Step 6

Critical temperature: why conviction keeps growing

Think of water. When it is warm, it is liquid and the molecules move freely. Cool it below 0°C and it freezes into an orderly crystal. The point where this change happens is called the critical temperature.

AI groups show a similar change:

TemperatureWhat the group does
Very highEvery agent votes at random; the average position stays near 0, and the group is undecided.
Very lowThe group locks into an orderly state (consensus or polarization) and barely moves.
Near the critical temperatureThe group swings between the two states, and fluctuations are largest.

The paper finds the critical temperature by measuring how much the average position fluctuates (χ): the temperature with the largest fluctuation is the critical temperature. Press the button to sweep through temperatures using the simulator's current parameters.

Takes about 1–2 seconds
Intuition

The paper finds that for every model, the actual operating point (T = 1) lies below the critical temperature, as if the water had already frozen. That is why discussion keeps making groups more orderly and more convinced instead of leaving them undecided.

Step 7

How accurate is the model

How do we judge a prediction? Agents rarely change their minds, so simply copying last round's answer already scores high on ordinary accuracy. The paper uses balanced accuracy instead: it sorts cases into four groups (changed or not, agree or disagree), computes accuracy in each, and averages them. Under this metric, copying and random guessing both score 50.

Political questions, networks seen in training, next-round prediction. Data from Table 1 of the paper (p.12).

Remember

The three-coupling model scores 75–86 in balanced accuracy for next-round prediction, and 61–77 when rolled out from round 0 all the way to round 8. On network types it has never seen, next-round prediction reaches 85–98. On a group of 64 newer models (a mix of GPT-5.6 and DeepSeek-V4), it still works, scoring 80–82.

Step 9

Open questions

The authors list their own limitations: one two-choice question at a time; a fixed network; agents without memory; and most importantly, the model discards what the messages actually say and keeps only positions.

From a security perspective, several gaps matter even more:

GapWhy it matters
Every agent is honestNobody lies, manipulates, or colludes. In practice, an attacker could join the group.
"Friend" is just a label in a promptAnyone could pose as a friend and borrow the strongest pull in the system.
The model ignores message contentYet attacks work through content, for example by arguing for a wrong answer with great confidence.
Questions were filtered for high uncertaintyOnly questions the models were least sure about were kept, so the results lean toward situations where agents are easily swayed.
Agents give gut answersThe paper does not test whether agents that reason step by step are just as easy to influence.

An extension the paper did not run: add a few adversarial agents

If group behavior is predictable, it can also be manipulated. In the math setting below, we add k adversarial agents. They always back the wrong answer, and each one poses as a "friend" to 4 honest agents. Each case is repeated 60 times, and we count how often the honest majority ends up correct.

The comparison is a simple defense: if a "friend" label cannot be verified, it counts only as an ordinary connection (β₀ only, no β⁺). This corresponds to authenticating relationships with cryptographic signatures.

This is an illustrative simulation based on the paper's update rule, meant to frame a research question. It is not a result from the paper.

Questions this raises

If the collective behavior of agents is predictable, it is also exploitable. The paper's trust edges are just labels in a prompt. How many adversarial agents would it take to flip a consensus, and would authenticating those edges raise that cost?

Step 10

Glossary and quiz

Agent

A language model with a persona that votes and sends messages.

Opinion ō

The average of 5 votes, from −1 to +1.

Group opinion n

The average opinion of all 32 agents; shows which side the group leans to.

Conviction c

The average squared opinion; measures certainty regardless of direction.

Network J

+1 friends, −1 rivals, 0 unconnected.

Intrinsic field g

An agent's own leaning, set by its persona and the question.

Couplings β⁺ β⁻ β₀

The strength of influence from friends, from rivals, and from simply being connected.

Critical temperature

The point where a group shifts from undecided to orderly.

Balanced accuracy

Accuracy averaged over four kinds of cases, so copying and guessing both score 50.
